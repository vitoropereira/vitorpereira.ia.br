There is something nobody tells you about ingestion pipelines: the most expensive
problem is not volume. It is finding out, three days later, that you processed
everything wrong — and no longer having the original to reprocess.

This is the story of how our queue evolved in two phases, and why the second phase
— the one that looks like a technical downgrade — is the one still standing.

## Phase 0: writing straight to the database

The beginning was the obvious thing: a message arrives, we process it and store
it. No queue, no buffer.

It worked until it did not. A WhatsApp group does not deliver constant volume — it
delivers in bursts. It sits quiet, and then someone drops an audio message into a
200-person argument and 400 messages arrive in two minutes. Multiply that by
thousands of groups and the peak looks nothing like the average.

The classic symptom: the database write becomes the bottleneck, connections pile
up, and what should have been a two-minute spike turns into half an hour of
degradation.

The diagnosis here is pretty direct: **the arrival rate is not controlled by you,
and the write rate is.** When the two are coupled, whoever posts in the group is
the one in charge of your database.

## Phase 1: RabbitMQ

The canonical answer to that problem is a queue, and we went with RabbitMQ.

It worked for what it set out to do. The spike stopped hitting the database
directly, consumption became a rate we controlled, the operation stopped degrading
during bursts.

But two costs showed up that I had not sized properly:

**One more piece to operate.** RabbitMQ is one more service with its own uptime,
its own memory, its own configuration, its own failure mode. On a small team, every
new component in the infra carries a recurring attention cost that does not show up
on the day you decide.

**The message disappears after the ack.** This is the point that matters. A queue
is a transport mechanism: delivered, processed, done. It is excellent at
guaranteeing the message reaches the consumer. It was not built to answer "what
exactly arrived here last Tuesday?".

And that was exactly the question we kept asking.

## The real problem was not throughput. It was reprocessing.

Every time we changed a parsing rule, fixed a classification bug, or adjusted how a
message type was interpreted, the same question came up: **what about the data that
already went through here processed by the wrong rule?**

With direct writes, the answer was: gone. You have the result of the processing,
not the input. If the processing was wrong, the data is wrong and there is nothing
to rebuild it from.

With RabbitMQ, the answer was the same. The queue had already acked and forgotten.

In other words: we had solved the throughput problem and still had the problem that
hurt most day to day. A parsing bug is not a rare event — it is normal life for
anyone ingesting third-party data in a format that changes without notice.

## Phase 2: the queue lives in Postgres, and keeps the raw data

The decision was to swap RabbitMQ for a queue inside Supabase/Postgres itself, with
one central rule:

> Store the **raw** payload, exactly as it arrived, before any processing.
> Processing is a later, repeatable step.

It is not a queue in the broker sense. It is a table of received events, with the
original JSON, an arrival timestamp, and a processing state.

What that changes in practice:

| Situation | Traditional queue | Queue with raw payload |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parsing bug found later | data lost, no rebuild | reprocess from the source |
| Business rule changed | applies going forward only | reapply across history |
| "Why does this record look like this?" | inference | read what actually arrived |
| Volume spike | absorbs it | absorbs it |
| Infra to operate | +1 service | zero — we already had Postgres |

The right-hand column describes our day to day. The left-hand one describes the
architecture the book recommends.

## "But Postgres is not a queue"

That is the right objection, and it has a clear limit.

A queue in a relational database has a real ceiling. At very high throughput, with
many concurrent consumers, you will run into lock contention, vacuum, and bloat —
and at that point a dedicated broker is the right tool, no argument.

What I disagree with is the leap people make from there: assuming you are at that
point before measuring.

We were not. And, importantly: **we still are not.** This architecture performs well
to this day. The theoretical ceiling exists and has never been touched.

What we gained was asymmetric:

- **Less infra.** Postgres already existed, already had backups, already had
  monitoring, was already in the team's mental model.
- **Real transactions.** Storing the event and updating the derived state in the
  same transaction eliminates an entire class of inconsistency between broker and
  database.
- **Debugging with the original in hand.** "This looks odd" stops being
  archaeology. You read what arrived.
- **Reprocessing as a routine operation**, not as a project.

## The idea that remains

If I had to reduce this to one sentence, it would not be "use Postgres as a queue".
It would be:

> **Separate receiving from interpreting.** Receiving has to be dumb, fast, and
> final. Interpreting has to be repeatable.

When those two steps are glued together, every rule fix is a data loss. When they
are separate, a rule fix becomes a reprocessing job — annoying, but recoverable.

And the technology choice — Rabbit, Kafka, SQS, a table in Postgres — is the second
question. The first is whether you still have what arrived.

In most pipelines I have seen up close, the answer is no. And that is what costs
you, not throughput.

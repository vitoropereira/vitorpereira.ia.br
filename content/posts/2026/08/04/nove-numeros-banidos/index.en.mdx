The alert was not "the numbers have been banned". It was a boring chart: message
ingestion had dropped to zero.

We ran a SaaS for WhatsApp group analytics. Ten numbers connected through an
unofficial API, pulling messages from thousands of groups. Overnight, **nine of
the ten were banned**. Not in a cascade, over the course of a week, giving us time
to react — all at once.

This post is about the two months that followed. It is not a hero story. It is
about what happens when the entire platform you built rests on a premise you do
not control, and that premise evaporates in the middle of the night.

## The mistake was not the ban. It was how we found out.

We knew the ban was possible. That is what an unofficial API is: you operate
somewhere the owner of the house can throw you out at any moment, and one day they
do. A known risk, consciously accepted.

The real problem was **how** we found out.

There was no alert on the ingestion rate. No monitor asking "is the message volume
of the last hour within what we expect for this time of day?". We found out by
looking at a dashboard for another reason and finding the flat line odd.

That is the lesson I carry most strongly from this episode, and it has nothing to
do with WhatsApp:

> If the health of your product depends on a number, that number needs an alarm.
> Not a dashboard — an alarm. A dashboard is something somebody has to remember to
> look at.

A chart that is only seen when someone decides to look is not observability. It is
decoration.

## Two decisions in the first few days

With nine numbers down, the obvious question was: how do we get the numbers back?
We spent almost no time on it. Because the right question was a different one:
**why does the entire product die when one supplier blocks us?**

That made it clear we had two problems stacked on top of each other, not one.

**Problem 1 — the operation was on Bubble.** The product had been born no-code,
which was absolutely the right call for validating it. But by then we were trying
to do serious data engineering in a tool that was not built for it. Every schema
change was an exercise in patience. We could not move fast — and we needed to move
fast.

**Problem 2 — the product was "group summaries".** In other words: the value we
delivered depended on reading every message, live, continuously. The product was,
by definition, hostage to the ingestion channel.

Nobody decides to rewrite the stack and pivot the product at the same time for
sport. We decided to because the two problems were the same problem seen from
different angles: the architecture and the value proposition were both tied to one
fragile dependency.

## The two months

We moved 100% of the operation from **Bubble → Next.js + Supabase**. And we
pivoted from **group summaries → group analytics**.

The difference between the two products sounds subtle and is not. A summary is a
continuous service: if ingestion stops today, the product does not deliver today.
Analytics is about the accumulated picture: patterns, trends, who engages, what
changes over time. An ingestion outage becomes a gap in the chart — not a product
outage.

The pivot was not cosmetic. It was **turning the most fragile dependency in the
operation into something that degrades quality, instead of a switch that turns the
product off**.

What helped it fit into two months:

- **Not migrating everything.** We migrated what supported the new product. A lot
  of Bubble simply did not come along, because it served the old product.
- **Accepting debt with a date on it.** Several decisions from that period were
  explicitly temporary. Some became permanent — and that is fine, they worked.
- **Keeping customers active the whole time.** That is not a detail, it is the
  constraint that defined everything. It was not a rewrite with the product
  switched off.

## What I would take to any stack

Three things that survived this episode and have nothing WhatsApp-specific about
them:

**1. Alarm on the flow, not just on the error.** Everybody monitors 500s. Far
fewer people monitor absence: the queue that stopped filling, the webhook that
stopped arriving, the cron that runs but processes nothing. A silent failure does
not raise an exception. It draws a flat line on a chart nobody is watching.

**2. Ask what dies along with it.** For every external dependency, the question is
not "what if it goes down?" — it is "what exactly stops working for the customer
when it goes down, and for how long?". If the answer is "everything, immediately",
you do not have a dependency. You have a single point of failure with a vendor
contract.

**3. No-code is great until the question changes.** Bubble was the right choice for
validating whether anyone wanted this. It became the wrong choice once the question
turned into "how does this hold up under volume and change fast?". That is not a
criticism of no-code — it is about recognizing the moment when the tool that got
you here does not take you further.

## What I do not recommend

Rewriting the stack and pivoting the product at the same time. We did it because
the alternative was worse, not because it is a good idea in the abstract.

If you are reading this without a crisis on top of you, do it in the boring order:
first find out which dependency kills you, then untie the product from it, then
touch the stack if it still makes sense. In that order you have time to make
mistakes.

We did not.
